# Career Path Clarity Assessment
### For College Students in Mathematics, Business Information Systems (BIS/MIS), and Computer Science

**Time to complete:** 15–20 minutes
**Total possible score:** 100 points (25 questions × 4 points)

---

## How to Use This Assessment

1. **Part 1 (Scored):** Answer all 25 questions. Circle one letter per question.
2. **Part 2 (Unscored):** Complete the Field Fit Sorter to see which of the three paths matches your natural preferences.
3. **Score it:** Use the scoring key at the end. A = 4 pts, B = 3 pts, C = 2 pts, D = 1 pt.
4. **Read your level and your weakest section.** The weakest section tells you what to work on next — that matters more than the total.

There are no wrong answers in Part 2. Part 1 measures **how far along you are**, not how smart you are. A first-semester freshman scoring 40 is exactly where they should be.

### Example
A sophomore answers: Section A = 15, B = 12, C = 16, D = 7, E = 13. **Total = 63 → Level 3, Narrowing Down.** Her lowest section is D (Experience), so her single highest-value next step is applying for a summer internship and setting up two informational interviews — not more self-reflection.

---

## What Your Level Means

**Level 5 — Focused & Launch-Ready (88–100)**
You know what you want, why, and what it takes. Shift from exploring to competing: interview prep, portfolio polish, targeted applications, and negotiation. Consider mentoring a younger student — teaching will expose any remaining gaps.

**Level 4 — On Track (71–87)**
Solid direction with a few missing pieces. Identify your two lowest sections and close them this semester. Most students at this level are missing either real-world experience (Section D) or a concrete timeline (Section E).

**Level 3 — Narrowing Down (54–70)**
You have a general direction but haven't tested it against reality. Your priority is *contact with the actual work*: one project, one internship application, and two conversations with professionals. Reality-testing beats more reading.

**Level 2 — Early Explorer (37–53)**
Normal for first- and second-year students. Focus on exposure and elimination. Take an intro course in each of the three fields if your schedule allows, attend club meetings, and complete a formal interest inventory. You're not behind — you're gathering data.

**Level 1 — Undecided (25–36)**
Start with self-knowledge before career research. Book a career services appointment this month, take a strengths assessment, and complete Part 2 below. Also worth asking honestly: did you choose this major, or did someone choose it for you?

**1. The part of a problem I like most:**

**7. Weekend project that sounds fun:**

**8. Documentation and meetings are:**

### Reading Your Tally

| Result | Suggested Direction |
|---|---|
| Mostly **M** | **Mathematics** — actuarial science, statistics, operations research, quantitative finance, cryptography, academia |
| Mostly **B** | **Business Information Systems / MIS** — business analysis, systems analysis, IT project management, ERP consulting, product ownership |
| Mostly **C** | **Computer Science** — software engineering, security, cloud/infrastructure, systems, ML engineering |
| **M + C** tie | Data science, machine learning engineering, quantitative development, computational research |
| **B + C** tie | Data engineering, solutions architecture, technical product management, analytics engineering |
| **M + B** tie | Business analytics, operations research, actuarial/risk consulting, revenue or pricing analysis |
| Even three-way | Analytics or data science sits at the center of all three — a strong default while you keep exploring |

**Important:** A tie is good news, not indecision. The three fields overlap heavily, and the most employable graduates usually sit in an overlap zone. Your major matters less than the combination of skills you can demonstrate.
